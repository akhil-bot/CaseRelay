# CASA (Court Appointed Special Advocates) — Domain Research

> **Scope:** Research overview of the U.S. Court Appointed Special Advocates (CASA/GAL) model, focused on organizational hierarchy, advocate-to-child/case relationships, workflows, responsibilities, and implications for designing a CASA technology platform.
>
> **Important:** CASA/GAL structures, terminology, assignment rules, and legal responsibilities can vary by state and local program. This document describes the common National CASA/GAL model and should not be treated as legal advice.

## 1. What is CASA?

**CASA** stands for **Court Appointed Special Advocate**.

CASA/GAL volunteers advocate for the **best interests of children who have experienced abuse or neglect and are involved in the court/child-welfare system**.

A judge appoints a CASA/GAL volunteer to advocate for the child's best interests. The volunteer works with legal and child-welfare professionals, educators, service providers, caregivers, and other relevant people so the court has better information for decision-making.

The core objective is:

- Child safety
- Stability
- Appropriate care and services
- Family/relationship connections where safe
- Permanency
- The opportunity for the child to thrive

National CASA/GAL states that volunteers generally remain involved from appointment until the case closes and the child reaches permanency.

Sources:
- National CASA/GAL — The CASA/GAL Model
- National CASA/GAL — Role of National CASA/GAL

## 2. Why CASA exists

The CASA model was created because judges may have to make major decisions affecting abused or neglected children without having enough direct information about the child's circumstances.

The first CASA program was created in 1977 in King County, Washington, by Judge David Soukup.

The basic idea was to use carefully screened and trained community volunteers to become a consistent source of information and advocacy for the child.

The volunteer can spend significant time understanding the child's circumstances and then provide information and recommendations to the court.

Importantly:

> The CASA/GAL advocate does **not** make the final decision about the child's future. The judge does.

Sources:
- National CASA/GAL — Resources for Judges
- National CASA/GAL — Measuring the impact of the CASA/GAL model

## 3. CASA vs. GAL

The terminology varies by jurisdiction.

- **CASA** = Court Appointed Special Advocate
- **GAL** = Guardian ad Litem

Both can involve court-appointed advocacy for the best interests of children who have experienced abuse or neglect, but the exact legal role and terminology can differ by location.

When referring to the role generically, **Advocate** is the safer term, with the jurisdiction-specific designation noted alongside it.

## 5. Does a CASA advocate have a supervisor?

### Yes.

CASA volunteers are generally supported and supervised by trained program staff.

A common staff role is:

**Advocate Supervisor / Advocacy Supervisor / Volunteer Supervisor / Case Manager**

The exact title varies between programs.

Typical supervisor responsibilities include:

- Supporting and guiding volunteer advocates
- Reviewing assigned cases
- Ensuring volunteers follow program policies
- Helping advocates prepare court reports
- Helping advocates meet court-related responsibilities
- Reviewing case documentation
- Supporting communication with child welfare agencies and courts
- Helping resolve issues or escalate concerns
- Providing ongoing supervision and training

Example structure:

```text
Program Manager
       │
       ▼
Advocate Supervisor
       │
       ├── Advocate A
       │     ├── Case 101
       │     └── Case 102
       │
       ├── Advocate B
       │     └── Case 103
       │
       └── Advocate C
             └── Case 104
```

A real local program example describes an Advocate Supervisor reporting to a Program Manager and supervising approximately 25–30 volunteer advocates, while supporting their assigned cases. Actual caseload limits vary by program.

## 6. How is an advocate mapped to children?

This is an important domain distinction.

A CASA advocate does **not** hold a permanent one-to-one relationship with a single child.

National CASA/GAL states that volunteers are generally assigned to **one or two children or sibling groups at a time**.

The important relationship is:

```text
Advocate
   │
   ▼
Assignment
   │
   ▼
Case
   │
   ├── Child A
   ├── Child B
   └── Child C
```

All of the following occur in practice:

- One advocate → one case
- One advocate → multiple cases
- One advocate → one child
- One advocate → multiple children in a sibling group

The **assignment** — not the advocate or the child alone — is what carries this relationship.

## 7. Assignments change over time

An advocate's relationship to a case has a beginning and, usually, an end. A child's case may pass through more than one advocate, and an advocate carries different cases over their years of service. Treating the relationship as a permanent link between one advocate and one child loses that history.

For example:

```text
Advocate A
   │
   ├── Assignment 1 → Case 100 → Children A/B
   │                  2025-01 → 2025-12
   │
   └── Assignment 2 → Case 200 → Child C
                      2026-01 → Active
```

## 8. Sibling groups

Sibling groups are especially important.

Example:

```text
Case #123
│
├── Child A — age 12
├── Child B — age 9
└── Child C — age 5
```

A CASA volunteer may be assigned to the sibling group rather than treating every child as an entirely independent case.

The grouping matters:

```text
Case
   │
   └── Sibling Group
          │
          ├── Child A
          ├── Child B
          └── Child C
```

But each child still needs an individual profile because their needs, placement, education, health, relationships, and court requirements can differ.

## 9. What does a CASA advocate actually do?

The advocate's job is much broader than simply visiting the child.

Typical activities include:

### A. Understand the case

The advocate reviews:

- Court documents
- Case plans
- Placement information
- Previous reports
- School information
- Service records
- Relevant medical/behavioral information
- Other available records

### B. Build a relationship with the child

The advocate:

- Meets the child
- Develops rapport
- Understands the child's circumstances
- Understands the child's needs
- Learns about important relationships
- Observes the child's living situation

### C. Talk to people around the child

The advocate may communicate with:

- Child
- Parents
- Foster parents/caregivers
- Social workers
- Teachers
- Therapists/service providers
- Attorneys
- Other relevant people

### D. Monitor placement

The advocate can monitor:

- Placement stability
- Safety
- School attendance/progress
- Emotional well-being
- Relationships
- Services being delivered

### E. Monitor court orders and services

The advocate may check whether:

- Court-ordered services are happening
- Case-plan requirements are being addressed
- Required appointments are happening
- Recommended services are available
- There are delays or gaps

### F. Advocate for the child

The advocate identifies issues and advocates for appropriate services and decisions that support the child's best interests.

### G. Court reporting

The advocate may prepare reports for the court containing:

- Facts gathered
- Observations
- Current circumstances
- Progress
- Concerns
- Recommendations

### H. Attend hearings and meetings

Depending on jurisdiction and program rules, the advocate may participate in:

- Court hearings
- Case staffing
- Family team meetings
- School meetings
- Other relevant meetings

## 11. The advocate is not the judge

This distinction is critical for any CASA technology product.

```text
CASA Advocate
      │
      │ gathers information
      │ analyzes circumstances
      │ advocates
      │ makes recommendations
      ▼
     Judge
      │
      │ makes legal decision
      ▼
Child's Case Outcome
```

The CASA advocate provides information and best-interest recommendations.

The judge makes the final judicial decision.

An AI system should therefore **not automatically determine what is "best" for a child** or make placement/permanency decisions.

A safer product role is:

> **Decision-support and case-management assistance for human advocates and supervisors.**

## 14. Useful supervisor dashboard

A CASA supervisor dashboard could show:

### Portfolio overview

- Number of active advocates
- Number of active cases
- Number of children served
- Cases approaching hearings
- Overdue documentation
- Missing required contacts
- Cases without recent activity

### Risk indicators

```text
HIGH RISK
- No recent child contact
- Placement instability
- Court hearing approaching
- Court report overdue
- Service not delivered
- Case-plan milestone overdue
- Advocate has unresolved concern
```

The system should present these as **signals requiring human review**, not automated conclusions about the child.

## 15. Potential AI use cases

CASA is a potentially strong domain for responsible AI because advocates deal with large amounts of fragmented information.

### 15.1 Case summarization

Input:

- Court reports
- Case plans
- Contact notes
- School reports
- Service records

AI generates:

```text
Case Summary
├── Current situation
├── Recent changes
├── Open issues
├── Important dates
├── Services
├── Placement history
└── Questions requiring advocate review
```

### 15.2 Child timeline

Automatically construct:

```text
2025-01-10 → Case opened
2025-01-15 → Initial placement
2025-02-03 → CASA assigned
2025-03-12 → School issue identified
2025-04-02 → Service referral
2025-05-10 → Placement changed
2025-06-15 → Court hearing
```

This can help advocates understand long, complicated histories.

### 15.3 Court-order tracking

Extract requirements from documents:

```text
Court Order
     │
     ├── Parent service requirement
     ├── Child therapy requirement
     ├── School requirement
     └── Next hearing
```

Then track whether evidence of completion exists.

### 15.4 Missing-information detection

The system could identify:

> "The latest court report references a therapy service, but no recent service update is present in the case record."

This should be presented as a **possible information gap**, not a factual determination.

### 15.5 Advocate briefing

Before a hearing:

```text
Upcoming Hearing
      │
      ▼
AI Brief
      │
      ├── What changed since last hearing
      ├── Recent child contact
      ├── Placement changes
      ├── Open tasks
      ├── Court orders
      ├── Service progress
      ├── Unresolved concerns
      └── Questions to investigate
```

### 15.6 Supervisor briefing

For supervisors:

```text
Supervisor
    │
    ▼
Portfolio Analysis
    │
    ├── Cases needing attention
    ├── Overdue tasks
    ├── Upcoming hearings
    ├── Missing reports
    ├── Advocate workload
    └── Escalations
```

## 16. AI safety principles

This domain involves children, court proceedings, and highly sensitive information.

Therefore:

### AI SHOULD

- Summarize
- Organize
- Retrieve
- Identify possible missing information
- Create timelines
- Highlight deadlines
- Help draft reports for human review
- Help advocates prepare questions
- Assist supervisors with workload management

### AI SHOULD NOT independently

- Decide child placement
- Decide whether a parent is fit
- Determine abuse/neglect
- Recommend removing a child without human review
- Make legal decisions
- Override an advocate, attorney, social worker, or judge
- Treat inferred information as established fact

A strong design principle is:

> **AI assists the advocate; the advocate advocates; the judge decides.**

## 17. Key product insight

The most important problem is not simply:

> "CASA volunteers need an app."

The deeper problem is:

> **A CASA advocate must build a coherent understanding of a child's life from fragmented information across courts, social services, schools, caregivers, service providers, and repeated interactions — then turn that understanding into timely, fact-based advocacy.**

That creates several high-value opportunities for technology.

The strongest product direction would therefore be a:

**CASA Case Intelligence + Advocacy Platform**

rather than just a generic case-management application.

## 20. Key takeaways

### CASA is fundamentally about:

**Child → Advocate → Court**

The advocate acts as a consistent source of information and best-interest advocacy.

### Organizationally:

**National CASA/GAL → State Organization → Local Program → Program Staff → Advocate Supervisor → CASA Advocates → Cases → Children**

The exact staff hierarchy varies by local program.

### Assignment model:

Do **not** assume:

```text
1 Advocate = 1 Child forever
```

Instead:

```text
Advocate
    ↓
Assignment
    ↓
Case
    ↓
One or more Children / Sibling Group
```

National CASA/GAL states that volunteers are generally assigned to one or two children or sibling groups at a time.

### Supervisor model:

```text
Program Manager
      ↓
Advocate Supervisor
      ↓
Multiple CASA Advocates
      ↓
Multiple Assignments / Cases
```

### Most valuable AI opportunity:

```text
Fragmented Case Information
          ↓
       AI Layer
          ↓
Coherent Case Intelligence
          ↓
Human Advocate
          ↓
Better-informed Advocacy
          ↓
Court / Judge
```

The AI should remain a **human-in-the-loop decision-support system**, particularly because the domain involves children, legal proceedings, and highly sensitive information.
